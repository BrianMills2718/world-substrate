Estimate rules: some things in a world happen by chance, and Jev estimates the chance

Status (2026-11-10): complete draft. You approved planning it on 2026-11-10 ("i approve"), in parallel with the AI judge build. Nothing is built yet, and nothing on this page waits on you.

Why. You said, 2026-11-10: "im not saying jsut the judge uses jev. i can see there being a way where we have certain types of rules/contstatins that progagte base don jev". Today every rule in a world is certain: if its checks pass, its effects happen. An estimate rule lets an outcome depend on a chance that depends on the facts (a blade not serviced for two weeks is more likely to break today), asks Jev for that chance, rolls against it, and records both. Jev never decides what is true, and never decides what a person does.

This page is the plan's own package. The AI judge plan is on AI Judge Plan; the repository-wide package is on World Substrate: planning package. All share one set of checks, questions and models.

1. The examples: follow one day at the sawmill

Blue, solid: in the World Builder now Orange, dashed: added by this plan (planned, not built) Grey: what happens today

The sawmill. "A small sawmill: the saw cuts logs into boards while its blade is sound. The blade wears; the longer since it was serviced and the harder it is worked, the more likely it breaks. Mara, the sawyer, can service it, which takes the day. When the blade breaks, the saw stops, boards stop coming, and the buyer's order runs late. Logs come by cart across a ford, which is too deep to cross after heavy rain." Pick a case; the steps it passes through light up on the left. The numbers are an illustration of what the run page will show, not a run.

1. The rule's ordinary checkshave
A process the world runs each day (or a move a person tries) checks its conditions as today: the blade is sound and the saw is running. If a check fails, nothing is asked and nothing changes.
↓
2. Asked before in this run?new, E1
The same question with the same facts gets the answer already recorded in this run, so Jev is asked once per distinct question.
↓
3. Ask Jev a fixed question about named factsnew, E1
"Given the blade's days since service and yesterday's load, what is the chance it fails today?" Jev sees only those two facts, never what anyone said or thought. It answers a chance between 0 and 1. If it fails twice, the rule's own fallback applies (a chance the rule declared, or refusing the day), shown in orange.
↓
4. Roll against the chancenew, E1 (same seed as the skill roll)
The engine draws a number between 0 and 1 from the run's seed and this event. Under the chance: it happens. Never "it happens because the chance is high".
↓
5. Record both on the eventnew, E1
The question, the facts with their values, Jev's answer, the model version, the call's trace id and cost, whether it was reused, and the roll.
↓
6. The effects and every knock-on followhave
The rule's "if it happens" effects apply, and the same-moment knock-on effects run as for any rule, each step citing the step that caused it.
↓
7. People decide what to do about ithave
Mara's own resident decides whether to service the blade. No chance is ever estimated for what a person chooses.

4. Decided (the agent's calls, as working defaults)

Your words set the direction; these design points were worked out from reading the code and prior art (inferred by agent, 2026-11-10). None needs your answer; each can be vetoed.

#DecisionBasis
1An estimate is an input, never a fact. Jev's answer becomes an outcome only through the rule's roll; it never becomes world state by itself.Open question Q63's contract; the retired Jev note, "borrow estimators; own reality"
2Three kinds, matching Jev's three answer types: a chance roll (yes or no), a pick among the rule's own options, and an amount from the rule's own list of values. Always a roll, never "take the most likely" and never "happens if the chance is over 0.5".llm_client's typed decisions (NoulQuestion, ChoiceQuestion, ScoreQuestion); "Do LLMs Play Dice?" 2bRules and constraints. On a process (a rule the world runs) the outcome's effects follow. On a move (a constraint on what a person can do) a losing roll may block the move: it is refused whole, with the chance and roll named as the blocker.Your "rules/contstatins"; the judge plan's split, where a constraint is what the world makes possible or impossible
3What a rule declares: a fixed question, the facts it reads (nothing else reaches Jev), the answer type, the model, a required fallback (refuse, or a declared value) and the effects for each result. No chance roll without stakes.The skill roll's rules (docs/plans/open_attempt.md:188-194); architecture section 8: a missing authority refuses visibly unless a fallback is declared
4One seed shared with the skill roll. The roll comes from the run's seed and the event; whichever of the two features lands first builds the seed.docs/plans/open_attempt.md:192; open question Q33
5Replay never calls Jev. It reads the recorded answers and stops if one is missing, or if the rule was edited so its question or facts no longer match the record.The existing recorded-reply replay (scripts/replay_build.py:23-49)
6Ask once per distinct question per run. Same question, same facts: reuse the answer. Each day still rolls its own number.The rule names every fact Jev reads, so the same facts mean the same question
7When Jev fails: one retry, then the rule's fallback, shown in orange. Each try is cut off after 5 seconds.Measured: 1,330 of 20,001 recorded Jev calls failed (6.6%); network failures waited 20 s on average, while 99% of successful answers came within 2.8 s
8The free test run never calls Jev. When the World Builder test-runs a draft's rules with simple moves, a stand-in answers each chance 1 and 0 in turn, so every outcome of every chance rule is tried, for free and the same every time, and the record says "stand-in estimate (test run)". The cheaper options, and how they are weighed. Two designs cost nothing per run. A formula written into the rule (chance = 3% per day since service) would need arithmetic the rule language does not have. Declared chances need nothing new: the rule's checks pick a chance the rules writer wrote, for example under 5 days since service 5%, 5 to 9 days 20%, 10 or more 40%, rolled the same way; this is the same path a rule's fallback already takes. Against them: every number is the rules writer's guess, fixed when the world is built, and the numbers multiply with the facts (3 facts in 3 steps each is 27 chances to write); and you asked for rules that propagate based on Jev. That is an argument, not proof, so E1 compares them: if the rules writer's chances land within 0.1 of Jev's on 8 of the 10 cases, the rules writer is told to prefer declared chances for simple cases and keep Jev for the rest (Q88). Jev costs under a tenth of a cent per run. Speed matters more than cost: successful Jev answers averaged 0.43 s, but 6.6% of calls failed, network failures only after 20 s (measured). With a 5 s limit per try and one retry, a chance takes about 0.61 s on average and at most 10 s before its fallback, about 18 s over a 30-day run (worked out from those shares; Q85).

The plain version (planned for E1, not run). The workspace rule says an AI answer starts as the same model given the whole source. Here: Jev given the whole world rather than the two named facts. E1 compares, on 10 cases side by side, Jev with the named facts, Jev with the whole world, gpt-6-luna with the named facts, and the rules writer's declared chances (no Jev). The whole-world version is the record the rule asks for, not a design option: a rule never lets Jev read the whole world, because nothing could be reused and it could reach what a person said. If it tracks the facts better, the rule names more facts (Q83). A 10-question probe (about $0.0003) checks first that Jev's chance actually rises with days since service (Q82); the existing probe's chances were coarse (0.6, 0.55). No Jev call was made for planning.

Could Jev serve the judge's plain-words stopping point? Possibly, at about 1/50 of the judge's cost per check, but Jev would answer a chance, not stop or go, and has not been shown to read a whole world well. That is the judge plan's call for J5 (Q86); this plan only records the option.
